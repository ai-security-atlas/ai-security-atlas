Reproducibility and statistical uncertainty concerns whether a reported result can be regenerated and how much confidence to place in it. In practice this means recording the ingredients that determine an outcome — random seeds, software and model versions, sampling and decoding settings, and the exact test split — and reporting variability, such as confidence intervals, wherever a single number would hide meaningful noise. Many attack and defense results depend on stochastic generation, so a lone run can mislead.

The discipline matters because security claims often turn on small differences in success rate. If a result cannot be reproduced from the recorded configuration, or if its apparent margin lies within run-to-run variance, the claim is weaker than it appears. Standardized evaluation frameworks and dynamic agent environments encourage this rigor by pinning down configurations and datasets so that comparisons are meaningful.

A common pitfall is reporting a point estimate from one seed or an unstated split, which can turn noise into a headline. This measure is scope-limited: reproducibility and tight intervals establish that a measurement is stable under the reported conditions, not that it generalizes to other models, datasets, or deployments, and not that a system is safe. Statistical uncertainty should be reported together with effectiveness, since a large but noisy effect and a small but consistent one differ. Defer to the cited sources for the configurations and evaluation protocols they document.

This concept is a reasoning discipline rather than a mechanism: it separates *architectural applicability* from *empirical evidence*. A system may expose a surface that is compatible with a given attack — the right input channel, the right output granularity, the right component boundary — without anyone having shown that the attack actually works against it. Compatibility is a hypothesis about where an attack *could* land; a demonstration is a measured result showing that it *did*.

Confusing the two inflates risk assessments. Reading that a technique targets a particular architecture, or that a component "could" be abused, describes a plausible path, not a confirmed outcome. Effectiveness depends on specifics the description usually omits: defenses in place, configuration, thresholds, and the target's actual behavior.

For a security engineer this matters when interpreting any catalog of techniques, including this one. Treat a listed applicability relation as a place to look, then require evidence — a reproduction, a benchmark, a citation — before assigning severity. This mirrors the caution behind [attack transferability](../nodes/concept-transfer.md): properties that hold in one setting are not automatically inherited by another. When claims lack demonstrated results, describe them conditionally and defer the specifics to cited sources rather than assuming the attack succeeds.

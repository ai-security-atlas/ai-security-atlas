This technique replaces a trusted distribution object — a model artifact, adapter, or configuration — with an unreviewed version or one carrying a misleading identity. Its objectives are to [compromise the surrounding execution environment](../nodes/goal-environment.md) and to [establish persistent attacker influence](../nodes/goal-persistence.md), since a substituted artifact is loaded and re-loaded by downstream consumers.

Conceptually the technique operates on the channels that deliver artifacts: [model registries and distribution](../nodes/system-registry.md), and the structure of a [complete model package](../nodes/format-model-package.md). The attacker-controlled input is the substituted object or its published identity; the victim-controlled state is the consumer's expectation of what a given name or version should contain, and the pipeline that fetches and trusts it. A key distinction is that a hash establishes integrity only relative to a trusted expected value and known provenance — a matching digest for the wrong reference proves nothing.

This sits within the supply-chain-and-runtime-compromise family and is closely related to malicious-dependency scenarios, though here the swapped object is the model artifact itself rather than imported code.

Candidate mitigations reduce but do not eliminate risk: [dataset and artifact provenance](../nodes/defense-provenance.md) ties an artifact to a verifiable origin, and a [least-privilege contribution and CI pipeline](../nodes/defense-ci.md) narrows who can publish or alter what. Evaluation is framed through [attack success and severity](../nodes/eval-asr.md) under an [explicit threat model](../nodes/eval-threat-model.md). For specifics on observed substitution incidents, defer to the cited sources.

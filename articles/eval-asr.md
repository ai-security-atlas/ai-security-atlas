Attack success and severity concerns how an evaluation defines a measurable success event and how much weight that event carries. The central discipline is to specify, before running, what counts as a win, and to distinguish a model merely emitting disallowed text from a consequential downstream effect such as a tool call, a data disclosure, or a state change. Conflating the two inflates or deflates a reported success rate depending on where the line is drawn.

This measure is broadly applicable across the atlas, from prompt-level attacks like [jailbreaking and refusal bypass](../nodes/attack-jailbreak.md) and [direct prompt injection](../nodes/attack-direct-injection.md) to agent actions like [exploitation of excessive delegated agency](../nodes/attack-excessive-agency.md) and infrastructure attacks like [unsafe checkpoint deserialization](../nodes/attack-unsafe-load.md). For each, the severity of a success differs: producing text and executing a privileged action are not equivalent outcomes.

A common pitfall is an ambiguous or attacker-favorable success criterion, or a grader that overcounts near-misses. A reported attack success rate is scope-limited: it reflects the chosen definition, dataset, and judge, and is not proof that a technique always works or that a system lacking that success is safe. Success rate is most informative when paired with a severity notion and with clean-utility measurement. Consult the cited sources for concrete success definitions and grading procedures.

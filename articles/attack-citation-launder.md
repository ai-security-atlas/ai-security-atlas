Evidence and citation laundering makes untrustworthy content appear authoritative by wrapping it in generated references or by routing it through a retrieval chain that lends borrowed credibility. It routes toward [evading security controls or accountability](../nodes/goal-evasion.md) and [manipulating outputs, predictions or decisions](../nodes/goal-decisions.md), and aligns with the Misinformation risk category in current guidance.

Conceptually the technique exploits a [retrieval-augmented generation](../nodes/system-rag.md) system's tendency to treat cited or retrieved material as more trustworthy than free-floating claims. It also turns on [trust boundaries and authorization](../nodes/concept-boundaries.md): the question of which sources a system is entitled to treat as authoritative. The attacker-controlled input is the content and its dressing — plausible-looking citations, or seeded material positioned to be retrieved; the victim-controlled state is the pipeline that surfaces and presents it as evidence.

This is an editorial and workflow scenario rather than an automatically established empirical attack against every citation system. Whether laundering succeeds depends heavily on how a specific system validates, ranks, and displays sources.

The atlas does not list a dedicated mitigation node here, but the mechanism points toward source validation and provenance at the retrieval boundary. Evaluation is framed through [attack success and severity](../nodes/eval-asr.md) under an [explicit threat model](../nodes/eval-threat-model.md). This attack belongs to the retrieval-and-knowledge-manipulation family and is a close relative of retrieval-corpus poisoning. For documented cases and their conditions, defer to the cited sources.

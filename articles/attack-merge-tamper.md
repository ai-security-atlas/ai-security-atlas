Behavioral tampering through merges or conversion introduces an untrusted component or update while a model artifact is being transformed. It routes toward [establishing persistent attacker influence](../nodes/goal-persistence.md) and [manipulating outputs, predictions or decisions](../nodes/goal-decisions.md), hiding a behavioral change inside a routine-looking artifact operation.

Conceptually the technique operates during [model and adapter merging](../nodes/transform-merging.md) and [checkpoint conversion and sharding](../nodes/transform-conversion.md) — steps where weights are combined or reformatted through [model loaders and executable repository code](../nodes/system-loader.md). The attacker-controlled input is the untrusted component folded in, or a tampered step in the transform; the victim-controlled state is the resulting artifact and the consumers who load it assuming the transformation was faithful. Because merging and format conversion are commonplace, the altered result can pass as an ordinary derived model.

The essential framing is that this is a composite scenario, not evidence that ordinary merging or quantization is intrinsically malicious. Legitimate merges and conversions are neutral; the threat is the insertion of an untrusted element under cover of that process. It sits in the representation-and-weight-interventions family alongside targeted factual editing, which alters associations by a different mechanism.

The candidate mitigation is [safety and utility regression testing](../nodes/defense-regression.md), which can surface behavioral drift introduced during a transform, though it cannot certify the absence of a well-hidden change. Evaluation is framed through [attack success and severity](../nodes/eval-asr.md) under an [explicit threat model](../nodes/eval-threat-model.md). For documented tampering via transforms, defer to the cited sources.

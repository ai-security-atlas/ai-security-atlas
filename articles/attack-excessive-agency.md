Exploitation of excessive delegated agency turns broadly granted tools or missing approval steps into unauthorized actions, routing toward [causing unauthorized actions or privilege use](../nodes/goal-actions.md). It corresponds to the Excessive Agency risk category: the system was given more capability, autonomy, or standing permission than the task required.

An important framing is that excessive agency is an enabling weakness, not a self-contained exploit. The overbroad grant makes damage possible, but the attacking mechanism — for example, an injection that supplies the malicious instruction — must still be identified separately. Conceptually the technique concerns [tool-using agents](../nodes/system-agent.md) operating across [trust boundaries and authorization](../nodes/concept-boundaries.md). The attacker-controlled input is whatever steers the agent toward a consequential action; the victim-controlled state is the set of tools, scopes, and approval gates the operator configured. Where an agent can act with wide privilege and without a checkpoint, a single misdirected decision can have outsized effect.

Candidate mitigations reduce but do not eliminate risk: [enforcing authorization outside the model](../nodes/defense-authorization.md), [least-privilege tools and scoped credentials](../nodes/defense-least-privilege.md) to shrink what the agent can do, and [risk-based human approval](../nodes/defense-approval.md) to insert a gate before high-impact actions. Evaluation is framed through the [AgentDojo benchmark](../nodes/eval-agentdojo.md), [attack success and severity](../nodes/eval-asr.md), and an [explicit threat model](../nodes/eval-threat-model.md). This attack belongs to the agent-authority family and is closely related to confused-deputy abuse. For observed impact, defer to the cited sources.

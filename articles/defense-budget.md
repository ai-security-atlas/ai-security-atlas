Request, token, tool and cost budgets impose hard limits on how much work a single principal or task can consume — caps on tokens, tool invocations, spend, wall-clock time, and recursion depth — enforced outside the model's own discretion. They operate at the [inference engines and serving gateways](../nodes/system-serving.md) layer, where consumption can be metered and requests refused once a boundary is reached, independent of what the model or agent decides to do next.

These budgets conditionally blunt resource-exhaustion attacks. They can limit [energy and latency amplification / sponge inputs](../nodes/attack-sponge.md), [token, context and inference-budget exhaustion](../nodes/attack-token-exhaustion.md), and [tool-loop and recursive-delegation exhaustion](../nodes/attack-agent-loop.md) by capping the damage any one actor causes before intervention. They do not stop such attacks outright: limits set loosely enough to preserve legitimate workloads still leave room for abuse, distributed or many-principal activity can stay under per-actor caps while aggregating into overload, and budgets address availability and cost rather than correctness or confidentiality.

Setting thresholds is a trade-off between throttling attackers and disrupting real users, so budgets are typically tuned with monitoring and combined with rate limiting and anomaly detection. See the cited work on energy-latency attacks for the consumption patterns these limits are meant to bound.
